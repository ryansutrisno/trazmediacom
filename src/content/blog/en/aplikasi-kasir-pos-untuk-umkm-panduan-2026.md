Most small shops start with a notebook, move to a spreadsheet, and stop there because POS software feels too complex or too expensive. Yet the problems that keep appearing are simple ones: physical stock does not match the records, daily revenue is only known after closing, and cashiers cannot find a customer's purchase history.

This guide helps you choose a point-of-sale (POS) system in the right order: understand the job to be done, define the features you actually need, then compare options. The goal is not the most advanced software — it is a system your team uses every single day.

## TL;DR

- **A POS is not just a cash register.** Its value is consistent stock, reporting, and customer data.
- **Must-have features:** fast checkout, automatic stock movement, sales reports, multiple payment methods, offline mode, and cashier permissions.
- **Do not start from features.** Start from the store workflow: who opens the shift, how stock arrives, what happens on a return.
- **SaaS fits most SMEs.** Custom makes sense when the operating model is genuinely unusual, not just a matter of taste.
- **Pilot with real data for 7–14 days** before moving your whole operation.

## What a POS Really Does

Point of Sale is the moment a transaction happens, but a modern POS records the trail of that transaction. When a cashier confirms payment, the system should deduct stock, log the payment method, attach the customer where relevant, and update the daily report.

The real difference usually shows up after the transaction, not in input speed:

- **Stock updates automatically**, so nobody recounts manually every night.
- **Customer history is stored**, so you know who repeatedly buys which product.
- **Reports are consistent** because they are calculated the same way every day.
- **Shifts are measurable**, so cash discrepancies can be traced to a person and a time.

If you only need a receipt and a total, a calculator plus handwritten receipts is still enough. A POS starts to pay off when transaction count, product variation, or staff count grows.

## Must-Have Features for a Small Business POS

### 1. A fast checkout flow that survives interruptions

Cashiers work in a queue, and every extra click is felt by a waiting customer. Look for fast product search, barcode support, quick cash denomination buttons, and keyboard shortcuts if you use a computer.

Also test the uncomfortable scenarios: the customer changes their mind, an item is scanned twice, the payment is short, or a manual discount is applied. A good system handles corrections without breaking the report.

### 2. Inventory that is tied to transactions

Stock that is not connected to sales will always lag behind. At minimum, the system should deduct stock on sale, add stock on receiving, and record adjustments with a reason.

For shops with sizes or colors, make sure the product structure supports variants without duplicating SKUs manually. For businesses with ingredients, check whether recipes or unit conversions are supported.

### 3. Reports you can act on immediately

A useful report answers real questions: which products sold best this week, which hours are busiest, which payment methods dominate, and how much was returned. If the report only shows total revenue, you will keep building a spreadsheet on the side.

### 4. Multiple payment methods, including QRIS

Cash, debit/credit cards, QRIS, transfers, and split payments should be recorded within a single transaction. Make sure non-cash payments can be reconciled against your bank statement — this is the most common source of discrepancies.

### 5. Offline mode and synchronisation

Internet connections at a shop are not always reliable. Software that can keep selling offline and sync when the connection returns is far safer than software that stops entirely. Ask how conflicts are resolved when two devices sell the same item while offline.

### 6. Roles and audit trails

Cashiers should not be able to change prices freely or delete transactions. A decent system separates owner, supervisor, and cashier roles, and records who did what and when. This feels unimportant until the first cash discrepancy appears.

### 7. Backup and data export

Sales data is an asset. Make sure you can export transactions and products to CSV or Excel, and understand how often data is backed up. Avoid vendors that give you no way out of your own data.

## Nice-to-Have Features That Should Not Be Priorities

- **Loyalty programs and points.** Useful when customers genuinely return and you can manage the communication.
- **Multi-outlet and stock transfers.** Essential with more than one location, but added complexity for a single shop.
- **Marketplace and accounting integrations.** Time savers, but set them up after internal flows are stable.
- **An online catalogue.** Valuable if you want to sell through a WhatsApp link or social media.

A common mistake is buying the most complete plan and using 20% of it. A simple system used fully beats a complete system the team abandons.

## SaaS POS, Custom POS, or a Spreadsheet?

| Approach | Best for | Strengths | Trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spreadsheet | Early testing, very low volume | Cheap, flexible | Error-prone, not real-time, hard to share |
| Subscription SaaS POS | Most retail, F&B, and service shops | Fast to start, low upfront cost, mature features | Recurring cost, limited customisation |
| Custom POS | Unusual workflows, multi-branch, internal integrations | Follows your process, connects to other systems | Higher upfront cost, requires maintenance |

A practical rule: choose SaaS if your needs can be described in generic terms like "retail shop with stock and discounts". Consider custom when there are rules you cannot compromise on — tiered reseller pricing, agent commissions, or production that consumes raw materials automatically.

## How to Tell Whether a POS Is Worth Paying For

Do not start from the subscription price. Start from the cost of the problem you want to solve. Estimate monthly:

1. **Manual reconciliation time.** How many hours per week go into matching stock and sales records? Multiply by the value of that time.
2. **Stock leakage.** Compare physical stock against records for one month. That gap is a real loss.
3. **Lost sales.** How often do customers leave because checkout is slow, or because an item that should be in stock is not?
4. **Decisions made without data.** How many slow-moving products keep getting reordered because no report says otherwise?

If those numbers exceed the annual subscription, a POS is justified. If not, wait and fix the manual process first.

## Data Migration and Team Training

Switching systems is not just moving product data. Follow this order so operations do not stop:

1. **Clean the old data.** Remove duplicate products, fix prices, and settle on consistent units.
2. **Define categories and variants.** A tidy structure early saves thousands of corrections later.
3. **Import opening stock and run a stocktake.** Record the cut-off date and time so old and new reports do not overlap.
4. **Train cashiers with real scenarios.** Not a feature demo — simulate rush hour, returns, and cancellations.
5. **Run in parallel for 3–7 days.** Compare daily totals between the new and old system before abandoning the old one.
6. **Assign one data owner.** Without an owner, the product master data will be messy again within months.

## Common Mistakes

- **Buying on features instead of workflow.** The result is a complete system nobody uses.
- **Not testing offline mode.** You discover the gap when the internet dies during peak hours.
- **Ignoring product structure.** Messy variants and units keep reports permanently inaccurate.
- **Skipping role setup.** Everyone uses the owner account, so the audit trail means nothing.
- **Never exporting data.** Your data lives with the vendor, not with you.

## Vendor Checklist

- Can you trial the app with your own shop data before subscribing?
- What happens when the internet drops, and how does sync work afterwards?
- Do the available reports answer your daily questions without extra exports?
- Can you export all your data at any time?
- What is the total annual cost, including hardware, printers, and per-user fees?
- How does support work during your operating hours, and through which channels?
- What happens to your data if you stop subscribing?

## FAQ

**Does a small single-outlet business need a POS?**
Not necessarily. If you handle a few dozen transactions a day and stock moves slowly, a spreadsheet is still fine. A POS starts to help when stock stops matching or when more than one person handles sales.

**Is an Android POS better than a computer-based one?**
It depends on mobility. Android suits shops that move sales points or use inexpensive devices. A computer is more comfortable for many SKUs, complex reports, and routine receipt printing.

**Should a POS be connected to accounting?**
Ideally yes, but do not make it an early requirement. Stabilise transaction and stock records first, then integrate so the data you send is already clean.

**How long does POS implementation take?**
With ready-made software, usually one to two weeks including data cleanup and training. For a custom POS, it depends on workflow complexity and integrations, and is typically scoped per module.

**What if staff resist the new system?**
Involve cashiers in the selection, train with real scenarios, and show the immediate benefit to them — less manual reconciliation and faster returns. Changes that make their day easier get adopted faster.
